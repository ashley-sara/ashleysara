How AI is changing the job market (and what it means for your team)

By Ashley Sara on LinkedIn (opens in new tab) ·October 26, 2026 ·5 min read

The short answer

AI is reshaping jobs faster than it is eliminating them. The World Economic Forum projects 170 million new roles and 92 million displaced by 2030, a net gain of 78 million. The real shift is in skills: nearly 40% of what jobs require is expected to change, so the people and teams who learn to work alongside AI will pull ahead.

Every week brings a new headline: AI is coming for your job. AI is creating millions of jobs. AI is a bubble. If you’re a business owner trying to plan your next hire, or a professional wondering whether your skills will still matter in two years, the noise is exhausting.

So let’s skip the hot takes and look at what the data says in 2026.

  • +78Mnet new jobs projected worldwide by 2030 (WEF)
  • ~40%of job skills expected to change by 2030 (WEF)
  • 7.3%unemployment for recent grads, summer 2026, in line with prior years (CESifo)

The headlines vs. the data.

The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 surveyed employers worldwide. The projection: 170 million new roles created and 92 million displaced by 2030. That’s a net gain, not a collapse. But 41% of employers also said they plan to reduce headcount where AI can automate tasks, while 77% plan to upskill their people.

Researchers at MIT FutureTech describe AI’s effect on work as a rising tide, not a crashing wave. Their analysis suggests AI could handle most text-based work tasks at a “minimally sufficient” quality level by 2029, but the impact arrives gradually. That gradual timeline is good news: it means there’s time to adapt, if you actually use it.

And despite the scary headlines about new grads, a CESifo working paper using Census data found that unemployment for bachelor’s degree holders aged 22 to 25 was 7.3% in summer 2026, within the normal range of the previous four years. Other research using payroll data has found weaker hiring for young workers in heavily AI-exposed roles, so the picture isn’t settled. But “the entry-level job is dead” isn’t what the numbers show yet.

Where AI is hitting hardest.

The disruption is real; it’s just concentrated. Tech has felt it first. Nikkei Asia counted roughly 78,000 to 80,000 tech job cuts globally in the first quarter of 2026 alone, and attributed nearly half of them to AI or automation. Companies are rarely cutting “because AI replaced you.” More often, they’re reorganizing around fewer, more AI-fluent roles.

ZipRecruiter’s 2026 employer research describes the same pattern from the hiring side: companies are using AI to reshape teams and raise expectations, not simply to shrink payroll. In practice, that looks like:

  • Fewer purely repetitive roles: data entry, first-draft writing, basic reporting, simple support tickets.
  • Higher bars for the roles that remain: one person is now expected to do what used to take two, with AI as the second set of hands.
  • New hybrid roles: people who understand both the work and the AI tools that speed it up.

The skills that are rising.

According to the WEF, the fastest-growing skills are technical ones: AI and big data, networks, and cybersecurity. But human skills stay at the core: analytical thinking, resilience, flexibility, leadership, and collaboration. The winning combination isn’t “learn to code” or “be more human.” It’s both.

My take

AI handles the first 80% faster than ever. The last 20% (judgment, taste, strategy, relationships) is where people now earn their value. Train for that 20%.

What this means for marketing roles.

Marketing is a perfect example. AI can draft a blog post, generate ad variations, and summarize analytics in seconds. What it can’t do on its own: know your customers, decide what’s worth saying, catch the claim that would get you in legal trouble, or build trust over time.

The marketers thriving right now do three things well:

  1. Strategy over production. They spend less time making assets and more time deciding which ones matter.
  2. AI fluency. They know which tools to trust, how to prompt them, and how to check their work.
  3. Measurement. They connect activity to revenue, because “we posted a lot” is no longer a strategy.

Search itself is changing too, which creates a brand-new skill set: getting businesses recommended by AI assistants. I wrote about that in organic vs. paid in the age of AEO.

A 5-step plan for your team.

  • Audit the work, not the jobs. List the tasks your team repeats weekly. Mark which ones AI could draft, which it could finish, and which need a human.
  • Pick one workflow to automate this month. Reports, meeting notes, and first drafts are easy wins.
  • Write an AI use policy. What’s okay to paste into a tool? Who reviews AI output before it goes public?
  • Budget for training, not just tools. A tool nobody knows how to use well is just another subscription.
  • Hire for judgment. When you interview, ask how candidates check AI’s work, not just whether they use it.

Frequently asked questions.

Is AI going to take my job?

For most people, AI is more likely to change your job than eliminate it. The WEF projects a net gain of 78 million jobs worldwide by 2030, but nearly 40% of required skills will shift. Roles built on repetitive tasks are most at risk; roles built on judgment, relationships, and strategy are most resilient.

Which jobs are most affected by AI in 2026?

Tech has seen the most visible cuts, with tens of thousands of layoffs in early 2026, many tied to automation. More broadly, roles heavy in routine text and data work, such as data entry, basic reporting, and first-draft writing, are changing fastest.

Are entry-level jobs disappearing because of AI?

Not across the board yet. A 2026 CESifo study found recent-graduate unemployment was in line with prior years. However, other research shows weaker hiring for young workers in heavily AI-exposed roles, so it's worth watching closely.

What skills should I learn to stay competitive?

Pair AI and data literacy with human strengths like analytical thinking, flexibility, and leadership. Learning to direct and fact-check AI tools is one of the most valuable skills you can build right now.
